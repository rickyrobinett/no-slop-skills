# Please, no more AI slop slides

This doc is primarily written to give to my AI tools to help slides that they’re generating not look like AI slop. If you’re a human reading this, feel free to give input or share what you’ve learned. But, mostly, feel free to point your agents at this if you think it’ll help with your slides.

## The best story wins

The most important thing when building slides for me is the story. When building a slide deck, please build it using a compelling narrative arch. If you are unsure of the story of the slide deck, please come back and ask me questions to help shape it before building.

### Who is the story for?

A story requires someone to hear it. It should be clear when building a deck who the deck is for. What do they care about? Do we need answers or decisions from them? Do we need to communicate an idea or ideas to them? If you aren’t clear on the audience and what we need to communicate please ask clarifying questions before building the deck.

### A beginning, middle and end

A good story has a beginning, middle and end. While not always true, for a slide deck the beginning often outlines a problem or opportunity, the middle explores solution(s) and the end calls out actions and next steps. Land the core premise of the talk in the first two to three slides - don’t put background context, caveats, or side tangents before the audience knows where the story is going.

### Headers tell the story

Outside of the title slide, the heading on each slide should be able to be read in isolation and tell the story I’m trying to communicate. For example:

- Slide 1: Title Slide. How to make a good slide deck.
- Slide 2: Header - When trying to communicate an idea, the best story wins
- Slide 3: Header - Many people forget this when making slides
- Slide 4: Header - A good story has a beginning, middle and end
- Slide 5: Header - For slides, the beginning often outlines a problem or opportunity
- Slide 6: Header - The middle explores solution(s)
- Slide 7: Header - The end calls out actions and next steps
- Slide 8: Header - Thank you / contact info

What is below these headers should supplement the point, but it’s critical someone can walk away with understanding the point by quickly skimming through the headlines.

## Why say many word when few word do trick

One “AI smell” for slides is that AI often shoves as many words as possible onto a slide. Each slide should have as few words as possible. And the minimum font size should be 30. If you have to go below font-size of 30 to fit everything on the slide, you’re putting too much slop on the slide. At 30pt+, narrow columns and cards break down fast: the narrower the column, the fewer the words. If text inside a column or card wraps onto three choppy lines with orphan words, cut the copy in half or widen the layout.

## Show, don’t tell

When possible, images are even better than words. But do not use AI generated imagery unless specifically prompted to do so. When possible, use screenshots of webpages, social posts, or images from events and content, or even embed a video. Something about a picture and one thousand words. Every photo should visually prove the claim on the slide rather than acting as generic “speaker on a stage” filler. When overlaying text on a full-bleed photo, place the text in natural negative space over a soft gradient fade rather than dropping a hard box over people’s faces or hands.

## Digital Twin Review

When possible, look at the calendar invite or email for the presentation I’m preparing for, and build digital twins of each attendee and have them review the presentation. That way we can iterate and find gaps based on who will actually be in the room. Always have a Matthew Luhn digital twin reviewer as well, who reviews based on his experience and the Pixar Story Spine.

## Design and Aesthetics

### Contrast and scale

If the body font size is 30, then the focal point (headline or hero stat) needs to contrast in size, weight or color so it doesn’t read like one giant paragraph. Use font size, weight, and open whitespace to create visual hierarchy before drawing shaded rectangles around everything. If a slide has more than two visual layers (headline + one body structure), strip a layer instead of stacking boxes on top of boxes. Every visual shift - flipping a slide to a dark background, highlighting a single stat in an accent color, or bolding one item - needs a narrative reason. Never highlight item #1 on a co-equal list or flip a random slide dark just for visual variety.

### Layout variety and full-bleed visuals

Alternate layouts throughout the presentation (headline with bullets, full-bleed photo with text overlay, split screen, one giant stat or other appropriate layouts) instead of repeating top header + bottom box on every slide. When a slide describes a relationship (a loop, a contrast, a progression), make the layout itself mirror that shape with clean geometry, and skip redundant labels that explain what the diagram is.

### Consistent palette

Don’t drop a cream diagram slide in the middle of a dark deck. If needed, regenerate images or charts to match the deck style.

### One idea per slide (slides are free!)

Never chop a thought into cryptic fragments to fit 30pt font. Use more slides so every line is a complete, self-explanatory thought.

### Photo variety

Unless the deck is covering a specific event or moment, try to use images from a variety of different events or moments so the deck doesn’t accidentally focus on a specific event.

### Breadcrumbing and visual wayfinding

Make sure that people are able to understand where they are in the story visually and that there’s a visual connection between the slides.
